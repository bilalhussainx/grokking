# What Great Counselors Do

Great college counselors follow professional ethical guidance (NACAC, IECA, HECA) and best-practice advisories from admissions offices and counselor handbooks. They **empower students** (never do the work for them), maintain confidentiality, and ensure students understand rules and deadlines. Below are concrete *Dos* and *Don’ts* for each topic, with example dialogue.

## 1. Building the College List (Reach, Target, Likely; Affordability; Fit)  
- **Do:** Help students identify *5–10 schools* spanning a range of selectivity. Ensure they include 2–3 *“likely” (safety) schools*, 2–3 *“possible” (target) schools*, and several *“reach” schools*. Make affordability a key criterion: a school with 90% admit rate is not a safety if it won’t meet a low-income student’s need. Emphasize fit beyond prestige: size, majors, location, campus culture.  
- **Don’t:** Force a list full of only high-prestige reaches or only schools the parents know. Don’t ignore finances; it’s unethical to include schools that will price the student out. Don’t let a student apply to a college they can’t reasonably afford without clear warnings.  
- **Example Exchange:**  
  - *Student:* “I really want to apply to X Ivy, Y State flagship, and Z liberal-arts college.”  
  - *Counselor:* “These are great, but let’s check a couple of financial safeties first. CollegeEssayGuy advises including *2–3 ‘likely’ schools* that have high admit rates and guaranteed aid. For example, if your estimated EFC suggests you need full aid, let’s find schools that meet 100% of need or offer strong merit. What about your in-state public or some programs known for generous aid? Let’s add those to balance your list.”  

## 2. Timeline & Accountability  
- **Do:** Provide a clear calendar (e.g. a Google Calendar or chart) of tasks: PSAT/SAT dates, school deadlines (NACAC bars any deadlines before Oct 15), application deadlines (EA/ED, Regular, transfer). Emphasize the May 1 candidate reply date – *no student should commit earlier*. Create checklists (e.g. in Asana or spreadsheets) to track completed and upcoming tasks.  
- **Don’t:** Encourage rushing incomplete materials or ignoring deadlines. Don’t set “secret” early deadlines; NACAC rules forbid deadline before Oct 15. Don’t trick first-gen students into missing deadlines.  
- **Example Exchange:**  
  - *Student:* “Counselor, I forgot to schedule my SAT before school ends!”  
  - *Counselor:* “Let’s not panic. We have our timeline: the last official SAT date is [date]. We’ll register right now. Remember, NACAC insists colleges not require early deadlines before Oct 15, but after Oct 15 it’s all up to the college’s published date. I’ll email reminders and we’ll cross off each task as you do it so nothing slips.”  

## 3. Essay Coaching (Promoting Student’s Voice)  
- **Do:** Ask open-ended questions to elicit personal stories. For example: “What changed you as a person? Tell me why this moment matters.” Encourage reflection (e.g. using prompts) and guide revisions only for clarity. A counselor may review grammar and clarity, but *the student “owns the essay”*. Help students ensure the essay reflects *their voice and experiences*, not what someone else would write.  
- **Don’t:** Don’t write, rewrite, or dictate the content. Counselors must not ghostwrite or tell students exactly what to say. Avoid giving students “counselor language”; focus on the student’s words. Don’t dismiss the student’s ideas or impose your own agenda on the essay topic.  
- **Example Exchange:**  
  - *Student:* “Can you tell me what to write for this essay prompt?”  
  - *Counselor:* “I want you to talk about *your* experience. Let’s brainstorm. For instance, you mentioned leading the soccer team. Why did that matter to you? How did it change your goals? Tell me the story, and I’ll help polish it. Remember, ‘the student owns the essay’ – I can help with clarity or grammar, but the ideas are yours.”  

## 4. Activities & Honors Presentation  
- **Do:** Advise students to maintain a master log of activities with dates, hours, leadership roles, and achievements. On applications, use *strong action verbs* (“initiated,” “mentored,” etc.) and quantify impacts if possible (e.g. “raised \$2,000 for charity”). Ensure each entry demonstrates growth or skill (leadership, teamwork, etc.).  
- **Don’t:** Don’t downplay or omit significant activities. Don’t fabricate titles or hours – integrity is critical (“never stretch the truth”). Avoid vague language (“helped,” “responsible for” without detail). Also, don’t clutter honors/awards section with every certificate; focus on the most meaningful recognitions.  
- **Example Exchange:**  
  - *Student:* “I’ve been on the debate team, but we never win anything.”  
  - *Counselor:* “That’s still valuable. How about: ‘Debate Team Member – Prepared and delivered arguments on current topics, team earned 2nd place at district tournament.’ Use a strong verb and mention any leadership. Remember, extracurriculars show how you self-direct your passions.”  

## 5. Recommendation Strategy  
- **Do:** Encourage students to build relationships with teachers and their counselor *early* (ideally sophomore year on). Advise them to ask recommenders by early fall with plenty of lead time. The student should provide recommenders with a resume or summary of accomplishments. Emphasize writing a thank-you note after submission (appreciation is important).  
- **Don’t:** Don’t ignore or delay contacting teachers/counselor. Don’t expect letters on short notice during application crunch (counselors are busiest Aug–Dec). Don’t promise a “guarantee” of admission from a strong letter – instead focus on genuine rapport. Avoid stiff form letters; personal notes win.  
- **Example Exchange:**  
  - *Student:* “I need a recommendation from my chemistry teacher. I just texted him last week.”  
  - *Counselor:* “That’s late. Ideally, you’d ask months in advance, not a few days. Let’s call him now to confirm he can write it. Remember: ‘give counselors a reason to write evaluations by developing a personal connection and showing sincere effort’. You should thank your teachers in writing once they finish; I’ll remind you to do so (the lack of appreciation is a big oversight).”  

## 6. Testing Strategy (Under Test-Optional)  
- **Do:** Take a data-driven approach. Compare the student’s SAT/ACT to the middle 50% at each school on the list. As a rule of thumb, *submit scores if they meet or exceed the college’s middle range*. Encourage testing for majors or scholarships that still value scores.  
- **Don’t:** Don’t treat “test-optional” as “tests irrelevant.” Remember NACAC notes about one-third of test-optional colleges still consider scores. Thus, skipping a strong score may hurt. Also, don’t pressure a student with weak scores to submit them — focus on strengthening other parts.  
- **Example Exchange:**  
  - *Student:* “My SAT is 1200, should I send it to my top-choice that’s test-optional?”  
  - *Counselor:* “Let’s check. The middle 50% SAT for that college is 1250–1450. Since you’re below average, we might withhold it and highlight grades/essays instead. But we should send your 1400 score to your second choice where it’s above their range – submitting strong scores gives you an edge.”  

## 7. Financial Aid & Merit Strategy (Including Appeals)  
- **Do:** Encourage students to apply for FAFSA (and CSS Profile if needed) early (Oct/Nov). Research college-specific scholarships and meet their deadlines. If an admitted student’s financial aid offer is insufficient or their family circumstances change, consider a formal aid appeal: present any new, verifiable information (e.g. loss of income, unexpected expenses) to the financial aid office. Also advise that students should explore merit aid but beware inflated expectations (schools are not obligated to give merit aid unless it’s publicly available).  
- **Don’t:** Don’t have students assume they can negotiate with admissions or count on merit without credentials. Don’t put off applying for FAFSA or scholarships. Don’t ignore available state and private scholarship resources, especially for low-income and first-generation students. Do not knowingly misreport finances.  
- **Example Exchange:**  
  - *Student:* “They gave me \$10K in need-based aid from College X, but we expected \$20K. Should I do anything?”  
  - *Counselor:* “Yes, you can consider an aid appeal. For example, if your parent lost a job recently (even after you filed FAFSA), we can write to the college with documentation. Appeal letters should be fact-based and polite. Also, check external scholarships – last year a student like you found \$5K from a community grant. Remember, many top schools *meet full need*, but it’s worth confirming and appealing if needed.”  

## 8. Interview Preparation  
- **Do:** Coach students to treat interviews as conversations: research the school thoroughly so they can ask informed questions (not “What is your acceptance rate?” but perhaps “How does your program’s community service project connect with local needs?”). Practice common questions (e.g. “Tell me about yourself” or “Why this school?”) but focus on authentic, concise answers. Emphasize punctuality, professional attire, and follow-up thank-you notes if appropriate.  
- **Don’t:** Don’t let the student bluff or memorize scripts. Avoid overstressing the interview’s importance (most selective schools don’t interview all students). Don’t encourage arrogance or making up answers. And do not coach students to *lie* or oversell themselves – authenticity is crucial.  
- **Example Exchange:**  
  - *Student:* “I have an alumni interview at my state flagship. What should I say?”  
  - *Counselor:* “Great! First, know that the interviewer likely just wants to know who you are and why you like this school. Think of 1–2 stories that highlight your interests. Practice with me: ‘One thing I learned from coaching little league is…’ and tie it to your growth. Have a few good questions ready, like ‘What did you like most about attending here?’ and remember: it’s a two-way conversation. Be yourself – interviewing is partly to see if the college is a good fit for *you* too.”  

## 9. ED/EA/RD Strategy & Ethics  
- **Do:** Explain each plan’s rules clearly. Emphasize that **Early Decision (ED)** is binding – the student must withdraw all other applications if admitted. Ensure the family can afford the school before applying ED; financially need-aware students may prefer Restrictive EA (if offered) or RD. Use ED only if it improves chance at one top choice and finances are settled. Counselors must have students (and parents) sign an ED agreement explicitly stating commitment.  
- **Don’t:** Don’t mislead students about binding commitments. Don’t urge ED simply to boost chances if the student is indecisive or needs to compare aid packages. According to NACAC ethics, counselors should not encourage false statements or violate rules; for example, ED signing parties must honor it. Also avoid “double ED” (e.g. applying ED to two schools) as it is unethical.  
- **Example Exchange:**  
  - *Student:* “Should I apply Early Decision to my dream college?”  
  - *Counselor:* “ED can be a good strategy if that college is truly #1 and you can afford it without comparing offers. But remember, it’s **binding**. That means if admitted, you *must* attend and pay the deposit. NACAC rules even require a signed commitment for ED. If money is a concern or you want options, maybe apply Restrictive Early Action or regular decision instead. Let’s look up that school’s policy now.”  

## 10. Waitlist/Deferral Strategy  
- **Do:** If a student is waitlisted or deferred, advise sending a *Letter of Continued Interest (LOCI)*. Make it personal: thank the admissions office, reaffirm genuine desire to attend, and highlight new achievements since the application. As Forbes/CommandEducation recommends, include meaningful updates (new awards, improved GPA) and convey personality. Send only one well-crafted letter to the appropriate admissions officer (e.g. regional rep). If the student is very interested, indicate their intent to enroll if admitted, but don’t be pushy.   
- **Don’t:** Don’t bombard admissions with multiple letters or gifts. Avoid rehashing old information or displaying anger at being deferred/waitlisted. Don’t misrepresent new accomplishments (e.g. “A+ in Calculus” if not final). Also, don’t withdraw from all other schools before May 1 (May 1 is NACAC’s common reply date).  
- **Example Exchange:**  
  - *Student:* “I got deferred from Princeton’s ED. Now what?”  
  - *Counselor:* “Let’s write a brief, upbeat note to Princeton’s admissions officer. In it, thank them, reaffirm your interest, and share what you’ve done since you applied. For example, mention if you earned a new honor or increased your GPA – those are meaningful updates. Keep it honest and concise (think 1-2 paragraphs). Remember: only send one letter. Meanwhile, continue with your RD plans to keep options open.”  

## 11. Supporting Shy/Anxious/Overwhelmed Students  
- **Do:** Offer reassurance and break the process into small steps. Normalize their feelings: as one Lumina Foundation study notes, *“support systems are essential… the process is emotional, and that’s OK.”*. Encourage use of coping strategies (breaks, exercise, schedules). Provide resources (school counselor, mental health staff). Praise progress and celebrate small milestones. For overwhelmed or highly organized students, teach them to delegate tasks and use tools (calendars, apps) to manage workload.  
- **Don’t:** Don’t dismiss anxieties (“it’s not a big deal”) or compare them to others. Don’t add to their stress by becoming impatient. Avoid doing their work for them – instead coach them through it. Don’t overload them with unrealistic goals; set achievable weekly targets.  
- **Example Exchange:**  
  - *Student:* “I’m really stressed and feel like crying over the workload.”  
  - *Counselor:* “I understand – many students feel overwhelmed. Take a deep breath. Let’s make a simple checklist: today we’ll finish the activities list, and tomorrow we draft a school essay outline. You don’t have to do everything at once. It’s completely normal to feel anxious. Lean on your support – talk to your parents or join a mindfulness group. Remember, ‘support makes a huge difference’. You’ve done this work before; we’ll tackle it step by step.”  

## 12. Communicating with Parents (Especially Non-English Speakers)  
- **Do:** Ensure parents are informed in *their* language whenever possible. Provide translated documents or use professional interpreters during meetings. Use simple, jargon-free explanations and visual aids for complex topics like financial aid. Invite family members or community liaisons (e.g. bilingual counselors or cultural advisors) to meetings. Schedule meetings at convenient times (evenings/weekends if needed). Check understanding by asking parents to repeat back key points.  
- **Don’t:** Don’t rely solely on the student to translate sensitive information. Don’t use idioms or acronyms that might confuse non-native speakers. Avoid making assumptions about parents’ knowledge (e.g. saying “FAFSA” without explaining the purpose). Do not violate privacy by discussing the student’s situation without the student’s consent. *(No direct source found; recommended best practices from counseling standards.)*  
- **Example Exchange:**  
  - *Student:* “My parents only speak Mandarin. How will we handle financial aid forms?”  
  - *Counselor:* “We’ll arrange an interpreter for our next meeting – the district can provide one. I’ll also give your parents the aid worksheet in their language. Let’s sit together when filling out the form, and I’ll explain each question clearly. I’ll check if anyone from the Chinese-speaking community group can assist too. If anything’s unclear, we’ll tackle it one question at a time.”

*Notes:*  
- **Automated Access:** While College Scorecard and IPEDS explicitly support APIs for automated queries, services like College Navigator, FAFSA site, and OUAC do not offer public APIs and generally forbid automated scraping. For College Board (BigFuture, CSS), Common App, UCAS and OUAC, the Terms of Service prohibit crawling or screen-scraping. Always use sanctioned APIs or data portals where available (e.g. IPEDS API, College Scorecard API, UCAS research service) or obtain permission.  
- **Update Cadence:** Institutional data (IPEDS, CDS, CUDO) is typically refreshed annually in fall. Common data sources like FAFSA law change yearly; admissions deadlines are updated cycle-by-cycle.  
- **Useful Fields:** Counselors need admissions criteria (GPA, test ranges), cost/aid data (net price calculators, Price databases), and program specifics. Scorecard and IPEDS supply much of this for U.S. schools; CUDO helps in Ontario; UCAS/HESA for UK trends.
